What Is ADHD?

ADHD (Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder) is one of the most common neurodevelopmental conditions in adults. It is a neurodevelopmental disorder that affects attention, impulse control, and executive functions. It occurs in children and adults, across all genders and backgrounds. This page explains what ADHD is, how it presents across different life stages, how it is diagnosed and what treatments are available.

It is not being caused by personal weakness, poor parenting, or lack of effort. ADHD affects executive function. The brain systems are responsible for attention regulation, impulse control, working memory, emotional regulation, and the ability to plan and follow through on tasks. These are not things a person with ADHD chooses to struggle with. They are rooted in neurological differences in dopamine and norepinephrine signaling in the prefrontal cortex.

However, it remains significantly underdiagnosed, especially in women.

What does ADD mean?

ADD (Attention Deficit Disorder) is an older term. It was used to describe people with attention problems but without visible hyperactivity. It is no longer used as a formal diagnostic label in current clinical guidelines, including the DSM-5 or ICD-11.

Today, clinicians use the term "ADHD Predominantly Inattentive Presentation" to describe the same presentation. This subtype involves significant difficulty with focus, organization, and follow-through. But without the restlessness or impulsivity more commonly seen in boys.

The Three ADHD Subtypes

Not everyone with ADHD presents the same way. It is divided into 3 subtypes. ADHD is diagnosed in three subtypes, based on which symptoms are most prominent.

Subtype How it presents
01 Inattentive (ADD)

Difficulty sustaining attention, frequent mind-wandering, losing items, forgetting tasks, and being easily distracted. Often missed in childhood, especially in girls. This is the most common presentation in adults.

02 Hyperactive-Impulsive

Physical restlessness, difficulty staying seated, interrupting others, and acting before thinking. More commonly diagnosed in childhood.

03 Combined Presentation

A combination of inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive symptoms. Often considered the most clinically significant presentation in adults.

What are the causes and risk factors of ADHD?

ADHD does not have a single cause. It results from a combination of genetic, neurological, and environmental factors. No single factor causes ADHD on its own.

Genetics

ADHD is one of the most heritable conditions in psychiatry. It often runs in families. Having a parent or sibling with ADHD significantly raises the risk in other family members.

Brain development and neurobiology

Certain areas of the brain, particularly those involved in executive function, attention, and reward, develop more slowly or function differently in people with ADHD. The prefrontal cortex, which manages planning and impulse control, is especially affected. These are biological differences, not signs of damage.

Prenatal and perinatal factors

Exposure to tobacco smoke, alcohol, or certain substances during pregnancy is associated with a higher risk of ADHD in the child. Premature birth, low birth weight, and complications during delivery may also raise risk. These factors interact with genetic predisposition rather than causing ADHD independently.

Environmental factors

Exposure to environmental toxins such as lead in early childhood has been linked to attention problems. Significant early-life adversity, chronic stress, and trauma may also affect brain development in ways that worsen ADHD symptoms, though these do not cause ADHD in the absence of a genetic predisposition.

What does NOT cause ADHD?

Research consistently shows that ADHD is not caused by poor parenting, too much screen time, sugar, diet alone, or lack of discipline. These myths are persistent but not supported by evidence. ADHD exists across all cultures, socioeconomic groups, and parenting styles.

ADHD Across Ages and Life Stages

ADHD does not look the same at every age. The way it presents, the challenges it creates, and the support that helps change significantly across the lifespan. Below is a brief overview of ADHD at each life stage.

Children and adolescents

ADHD in Children and adolescents

In younger children, ADHD most often appears as visible hyperactivity. Like running, climbing, difficulty sitting still, and impulsive behavior in class. Inattention shows as failure to follow instructions, losing items, not completing schoolwork, and difficulty waiting for a turn.

ADHD in children may affect school performance, friendships, and family relationships. Children with ADHD are often described as "disruptive" or "difficult." Early assessment and support make a significant difference to long-term outcomes.

Adolescents who have not been diagnosed may be labeled as lazy, rebellious or unmotivated. Understanding ADHD can be beneficial both for the young person and for the adults around them.

Adults

ADHD in adults

In adults, hyperactivity may become less visible and feel more like internal restlessness. The most common adult ADHD difficulties include managing deadlines, organizing daily life, maintaining relationships, sustaining focus in meetings, and staying on top of finances and administration. Many adults developed coping strategies in childhood that mask the condition. It may lead to a late diagnosis.

Adult ADHD frequently co-occurs with anxiety, depression, sleep problems and substance use.

Older adults

ADHD in Older Adults

ADHD in people over 50 or 60 is a growing area of clinical awareness. Older adults with undiagnosed ADHD may have spent years developing workarounds and coping strategies. However suddenly stop working when life circumstances change. Such as retirement, bereavement, illness, or increased cognitive load.

ADHD symptoms in older adults can be confused with age-related cognitive changes or early dementia. A thorough clinical assessment is essential to distinguish between these conditions. There is no age at which it is "too late" to be assessed or to receive treatment and support.

Women and girls

ADHD in Women and girls: Symptoms, Late Diagnosis and Why It Matters

ADHD in women is one of the most underrecognized areas in mental health. For decades, ADHD was diagnosed almost exclusively in boys, with visible hyperactivity as a central feature. Girls and women with ADHD tend to present very differently. This means many go undiagnosed for years or are misdiagnosed with anxiety or depression.

The consequences of late or missed diagnosis in women are serious. Without understanding the neurological basis of their difficulties, many women internalise their struggles as personal failure. The chronic shame, self-blame, and exhaustion that result can lead to significant mental health difficulties, including depression, anxiety, burnout, and relationship problems.

ADHD Treatment. What Does a Psychologist Do After Diagnosis?

An ADHD diagnosis is not the end of the process; it is the beginning. The effective treatment can make a profound difference to your quality of life. At psykolog.no, our ADHD psychologists offer a range of evidence-based therapeutic approaches tailored to your specific needs.

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for ADHD (CBT)

CBT is one of the effective psychological treatments for ADHD in adults. CBT teaches practical skills to address the challenges of ADHD. A CBT program for ADHD might include the following:

Organisational and planning strategies: external structure systems, time management tools, prioritisation techniquesProcrastination intervention: breaking the avoidance cycle, task initiation strategiesEmotional regulation skills: managing frustration, impulsivity, and rejection sensitivityWorking memory compensatory strategiesSelf-compassion and reframing: addressing shame, perfectionism, and negative self-talk related to ADHD

Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT) for ADHD

DBT is particularly effective for the emotional dysregulation component of ADHD. DBT has strong evidence for ADHD, particularly for adults who struggle with rejection sensitivity, impulsivity, and emotional outbursts.

Metacognitive Therapy (MCT) and ADHD

MCT addresses the persistent worry, rumination, and "thought loops" that many adults with ADHD experience. It helps you change your relationship with unhelpful thinking patterns rather than fighting the content of those thoughts. MCT is available from several of our psychologists at psykolog.no.

Coaching and Psychoeducation

ADHD coaching helps you build personalised systems for managing the practical challenges of ADHD. Combined with psychoeducation, a deep understanding of how your ADHD brain works is crucial. This is often the most impactful form of support.

Medication (The Role of the Psychiatrist)

Psychological treatment and medication are not mutually exclusive. For many adults with ADHD, a combination is the most effective approach. At psykolog.no, our psychologists do not prescribe medication (only medical doctors and psychiatrists can). However, we work closely with psychiatrists and can refer you appropriately if medication is considered beneficial. We can also support you through the process of ADHD medication — monitoring effects, building complementary psychological skills, and adjusting your treatment plan.

ADHD and co-occurring conditions

ADHD often occurs together with other mental health challenges. Many people with ADHD also experience one or more other mental health conditions, some of which may appear first and mask the underlying ADHD. A thorough assessment at psykolog.no helps you get the full picture.

→

ADHD and anxiety

Many adults with ADHD also have an anxiety disorder. The constant effort to compensate for ADHD difficulties often creates chronic stress and worry. Symptoms overlap significantly, making diagnosis tricky.

 →

ADHD and depression

Years of underperforming despite real effort, repeated failures and low self-esteem make depression very common in adults with undiagnosed or untreated ADHD.

 →

ADHD and sleep problems

ADHD is linked to delayed sleep phase, difficulty switching off the brain at night, and restless sleep. Sleep deprivation in turn worsens all ADHD symptoms.

 →

ADHD and OCD

Both conditions involve executive function difficulties and repetitive thought patterns, though in different ways. Misdiagnosis between the two is common. A thorough assessment distinguishes them clearly.

 →

ADHD and autism (ASD)

ADHD and autism spectrum disorder frequently co-occur. Both affect executive function and social communication, though through different mechanisms.

 →

ADHD and low self-esteem

Chronic underachievement, being misunderstood, and years of negative feedback create deep patterns of low self-esteem in people with ADHD. Psychoeducation and CBT both directly address this layer of the condition.

Myths and facts about ADHD

Myth 01

ADHD isn't real.

Fact

ADHD is a well-recognized neurodevelopmental disorder supported by decades of scientific research.

Myth 02

Kids grow out of ADHD.

Fact

ADHD often continues into adolescence and adulthood, although symptoms may change over time.

Myth 03

Technology causes ADHD.

Fact

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition influenced by genetics and brain development. Technology does not cause ADHD.

Myth 04

ADHD is overdiagnosed.

Fact

Many people with ADHD remain undiagnosed or receive a diagnosis later in life. Accurate diagnosis requires a comprehensive clinical assessment.

Myth 05

People with ADHD are just lazy.

Fact

ADHD affects executive functions such as attention, planning, organization, impulse control, and self-regulation. It is not a sign of laziness.
