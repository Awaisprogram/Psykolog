Anger Management

Anger is a normal human emotion. Anger management is not about getting rid of it but about understanding it and responding to it in healthier ways. Anger becomes a problem when it is very intense, hard to control, or causes trouble for you or the people around you. It can affect your thoughts, your body, your behavior, and your relationships. A psychologist can help when anger keeps causing difficulties or feels impossible to control.

What is OCD?

OCD stands for Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder. It is a mental health condition. In which a person becomes caught in a repeating cycle. Obsessions (intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images, or urges) and compulsions (repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed to relieve the distress those thoughts cause).

The key feature of OCD is not the thoughts themselves. However, most people have odd or unwanted thoughts from time to time. The OCD person cannot dismiss the thoughts easily and experiences intense anxiety as a result.

Our psychologists specializing in OCD can assess you and give you a treatment plan if needed. In Norway, OCD is diagnosed using the criteria set out in ICD-10, the diagnostic manual used across the public health system.

Seeking help for anger issues is a sign of strength, not weakness. A psychologist helps you understand what drives your anger and gives you concrete tools to manage it better.

OCD Means

A chronic condition where unwanted intrusive thoughts (obsessions) drive repetitive actions. Also, physical behaviors (compulsions) in an attempt to neutralise anxiety, creating a self-reinforcing loop

OCD affects people of all ages, genders, and backgrounds. It commonly begins in childhood, adolescence, or early adulthood. Without treatment, OCD tends to worsen over time and can limit daily functioning, relationships, education, and work.

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

The obsession triggers anxiety, discomfort, fear, or a feeling that something terrible might happen.

Compulsion

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

The person performs a behaviour or mental act to reduce the distress, such as checking, cleaning, counting, or seeking reassurance.

Temporary Relief

WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE

Anxiety decreases briefly. However, the compulsion can reinforce the idea that the obsession was dangerous, causing the cycle to start again.

What Are Obsessions?

Obsessions are unwanted, intrusive thoughts, images, impulses, or doubts that cause significant anxiety. Common examples include fear of contamination, harming someone you love, or religious/moral wrongdoing.

What Are Compulsions?

Compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts driven by the need to reduce the distress caused by obsessions. They include visible rituals like handwashing and checking locks. The invisible ones can be mentally reviewing, praying, and reassurance-seeking.

Symptoms And Signs Of OCD

OCD is recognized by a pattern and by how much time it takes, not by any single thought or habit. Almost everyone has an odd thought once in a while. What sets OCD apart is how often the thoughts return and how much distress they cause.

Common obsessions include:

Intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images, or urges
Fear of germs, illness, or dirt
Fear of harming yourself or someone else, even when you would never want to (harm obsessions)
Religious or moral fears, such as worrying constantly about sinning or doing wrong
A strong need for symmetry, order, or for things to "feel right"
Persistent doubt
Unwanted sexual or taboo thoughts

Common compulsions include:

Persistent sadness or emptiness
Loss of interest in activities or relationships
Fatigue, slowed thinking, and poor concentration
Feelings of worthlessness or guilt

OCD In Children

OCD in children (OCD child) often goes unrecognised. Children may not be able to explain what is happening. They just know they feel wrong unless they do certain things. OCD can appear at a young age, with a peak onset in late childhood and early adolescence. It affects boys and girls, though boys tend to develop it slightly earlier.

Parents, teachers, and GPs often mistake childhood OCD for anxiety, ADHD, or autism. An early, accurate diagnosis from a child psychologist who specializes in OCD is essential for effective treatment.

Signs Of OCD In A Child

Repeating actions a certain number of times

Excessive handwashing or other cleaning rituals

Asking the same "what if" questions repeatedly

Becoming extremely upset when routines are disrupted

Avoiding certain people, places, or objects

Taking a long time to complete simple tasks (getting dressed, eating)

Declining school performance without an obvious cause

Expressing fears about something terrible happening to family members

How OCD Affects Daily Life

OCD does not just take up time; it shapes decisions. Someone might arrive late to work because a checking ritual took an extra twenty minutes. A student might rewrite an email five times because it does not "feel right" to send. None of these choices come from wanting to be difficult. They come from a brain stuck on a loop that keeps demanding one more check, one more review, one more moment of certainty that never quite arrives.

What Causes OCD?

There is no single cause of OCD. Here are the mix of factors:

Genetic and hereditary vulnerability. OCD can run in families.

Biological factors like differences in brain circuits involved in doubt and decision-making appear to play a role.

Family history of having a close relative with OCD raises the likelihood somewhat.

Environmental and psychosocial factors like upbringing and life experiences can shape how symptoms develop.

Stressful life events and transitions. A divorce, a new job, a loss, or another major change can trigger the first episode or make existing symptoms worse.

Sleep deprivation and stress both tend to make OCD symptoms harder to manage.

Is OCD Hereditary?

Genes appear to raise the risk of OCD, but they do not decide the outcome. Having a parent or sibling with OCD does not mean a person will automatically develop it themselves. Genetics is one piece of a larger picture that also includes environment, stress, and life experience.

OCD Treatment From Psychologist

Treatment for OCD is individual and often combines more than one approach. A good treatment plan is built around the person's specific obsessions, compulsions, and life circumstances and is adjusted as progress is made.

Exposure and Response Prevention

ERP is an effective therapy for OCD. It involves gradually and systematically facing feared situations (exposure) while resisting the urge to perform compulsions (response prevention). This teaches the brain that obsessive thoughts are not dangerous and do not require a ritual response.

OCD medicine

Combined treatment gives the long-term outcomes. Always in consultation with a prescribing doctor.

CBT For OCD

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy focuses on identifying and challenging the beliefs that give obsessive thoughts their power. CBT is often combined with ERP for a comprehensive approach.

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy

ACT teaches psychological flexibility. Accepting intrusive thoughts as mental events that don't require action while committing to value-based living. Effective for OCD, particularly when combined with ERP elements.

OCD and Relatives: Tips for Family Members

OCD not only affects the person who has it. However, it significantly impacts the whole family. Family members understand OCD and are guided by a clinician on how to respond. Then the treatment outcomes improve.

We offer relatives consultations separate from the person with OCD. To help families understand the condition, reduce accommodation, and know how to support their loved one effectively.

  • Express empathy without reinforcing rituals. Saying "I can see you're anxious, and I trust you can handle this" is more helpful than reassurance.
